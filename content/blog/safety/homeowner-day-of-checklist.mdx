There is a particular kind of relief that sets in when a booking is confirmed. You found someone, they were available, the date is in the calendar, and the problem that has been sitting on your list for the past three weeks is about to be someone else's responsibility for a few hours. It is an understandable feeling, and it leads directly to one of the most common mistakes homeowners make: treating the arrival of a professional as the finish line rather than the starting point.

Everything that happened before the booking, the vetting, the credential checks, the written scope, the quote comparison, all of that was preparation. The job itself begins the moment someone rings your doorbell, and the first ten minutes of that interaction carry more weight than most homeowners give them credit for. Not because something is likely to go wrong, it usually does not, but because the decisions you make before a single tool comes out of a bag determine how clearly protected you are if something unexpected does.

This guide walks through what to do from the moment a professional arrives to the moment the job is marked complete. None of it is complicated. All of it is worth making a habit.

## Why the Moment of Arrival Gets Overlooked

Most of the guidance homeowners receive about hiring professionals focuses on what to do beforehand. Get multiple quotes. Verify credentials. Check reviews. Read the contract. This advice is correct and it matters enormously. But it creates an implicit assumption that the work of being a careful homeowner is finished once the booking is confirmed and the professional is on their way.

The arrival moment gets overlooked because it feels socially awkward to treat someone who has just shown up to help you as a subject of scrutiny. There is a door between you and a stranger. You open it. They seem pleasant. They have tools. Everything feels normal, and the impulse to verify anything feels vaguely insulting to someone who presents as perfectly professional.

This is the impulse worth resisting. Not because the person at your door is likely to be a problem, most are not, but because the checks that feel unnecessary when everything is fine are the exact checks that matter when something is not. A professional who has been sent to your door by a verified platform, completed a full vetting process, and arrived to do a legitimate job will not flinch at any of this. They have done it before. They expect it. The ones who object or deflect when you ask reasonable questions before letting them into your home are giving you information you need before any access has been granted.

## Step One: Confirm the Person Matches the Profile

Open the TaskLync app before you open the door. Pull up the booking and look at the professional's profile photo. Then look at the person standing outside.

This check takes fifteen seconds and it is non-negotiable. The platform vets specific individuals. It verifies their identity, runs their background, confirms their credentials. All of that work is attached to a particular person whose face is on their profile. If someone different shows up, none of that vetting applies to the individual now asking for access to your home.

This situation is uncommon but it does occur. A professional might send a colleague, a subcontractor, or a family member to cover a job they cannot make, sometimes with good intentions and sometimes not. The platform's policy is unambiguous: the professional who accepted the booking is the professional who must attend it. Any other arrangement is a conduct violation, and the right response is to not grant entry and to report it through the app immediately.

If the photo and the person do not match clearly, do not let them in and do not feel obligated to explain yourself at length. A polite but firm explanation that you need to verify the booking through the app is sufficient. If the mismatch turns out to be a photo quality issue or an update that has not yet refreshed, the platform's support team can clarify quickly. If it turns out to be something else, you made the right call by waiting.

## Step Two: Verify the Booking Reference Out Loud

Before stepping back to let anyone past the threshold, confirm the booking reference number verbally. You have it in your in-app confirmation. The professional has it in theirs. Ask them for it, or read yours and ask them to confirm it matches.

This step exists because a booking reference ties the specific job, the agreed scope, the confirmed price, and the assigned professional together in a single verifiable string. A professional arriving for your job can produce it without any hesitation. A professional who cannot, or who tries to redirect the conversation rather than simply confirming the number, has introduced a question that deserves an answer before anyone crosses the threshold.

It also establishes, clearly and at the outset, that you are someone who pays attention to how the job is being handled. This is not about intimidation. It is about setting a tone that tends to produce better outcomes throughout the job. Professionals who understand from the first thirty seconds that a homeowner is organized and attentive approach the work differently than those who perceive an easy situation. The reference check is low-effort and carries more weight than it appears to.

## Step Three: Walk Through the Scope Before a Single Tool Comes Out

Once you have confirmed identity and matched the booking reference, the next step happens before any work begins. Walk through what was agreed, verbally and specifically, with the professional standing in front of the area where the job will take place.

This is not a renegotiation. It is a shared confirmation that both parties understand the same thing about what is being done, to what standard, and roughly how long it should take. The agreed scope is already documented in the platform from the booking, so this conversation is not creating new information. It is making sure there is no daylight between what the documentation says and what the professional understands they are there to do.

This step catches a surprising number of small misalignments before they become mid-job complications. A professional who understood the scope slightly differently than the homeowner, a detail that was not clearly communicated in the booking description, an assumption on either side about what was included. These are ordinary miscommunications that are easy to resolve in two minutes at the start of a job and considerably harder to untangle once work is already underway.

If the professional suggests additional work during this walkthrough, something they spotted that was not in the original scope, the right move is to get it quoted and approved through the app before agreeing to anything. Do not proceed on a verbal agreement for anything outside the original booking. Not because the professional is necessarily trying to overcharge you, many genuinely flag additional work in good faith, but because verbal agreements about additional scope are the origin point of almost every billing dispute that follows a job.

## Step Four: Photograph the Work Area Before Anything Is Touched

For any job that involves a surface, fixture, wall, floor, or fitting that could plausibly be the subject of a dispute, take photographs of the area before work starts. Do this while the professional is still with you, ideally, so that both of you have a shared reference point for what the space looked like at the beginning.

The purpose of these photos is narrow and specific. They document the pre-existing condition of the area being worked on. If a question arises after the job about whether damage was caused by the work or was already present, a timestamped photo taken before any tools were used is the clearest possible evidence. Without it, a dispute about pre-existing versus caused damage becomes a matter of competing accounts with no factual anchor.

For small jobs, a handful of photos from different angles covering the entire work area is enough. For larger jobs involving multiple rooms or areas, cover each zone separately. You do not need professional-quality documentation. You need photos that are clear enough to show the condition of the space and recent enough to be unambiguous about when they were taken. Your phone's standard camera is perfectly adequate.

Store these photos somewhere you can find them easily. Your phone's camera roll with the timestamp visible is fine. If you use cloud storage that auto-syncs, they are already backed up. The goal is to have them available if they are ever needed, not to create an elaborate filing system.

## Step Five: Keep Access to the Job Area Only

Once work begins, the professional's access to your home should extend only as far as the job requires. If the work is in the kitchen, the rest of the house is not part of the job. If the repair is in a specific bathroom, the other rooms are not relevant to it.

This is worth being deliberate about rather than leaving it to drift. Most homeowners invite a professional inside and then move on with their day, which is entirely reasonable. But it is worth taking a moment at the start to make clear, without any particular emphasis or awkwardness, which area is relevant to the work and to keep other spaces closed or naturally out of the flow of the job.

This practice protects both parties. The professional who accessed only the area directly relevant to their work cannot be reasonably associated with anything that happens elsewhere in the home, either during the job or discovered afterward. And the homeowner who maintained clear boundaries around the scope of access has a simpler picture of what occurred during the time the professional was present. It is a clean arrangement that removes ambiguity from a situation where ambiguity is rarely useful.

If the job genuinely requires access to additional areas, water shutoffs in a utility room for a plumbing job, for example, that access is practical and reasonable. The principle is to keep it deliberate rather than open-ended. Know what access is needed, grant that specifically, and treat anything beyond it as outside the scope of the visit.

## Step Six: Rate the Job While the Details Are Still Sharp

When the work is done and the professional has left, open the app and submit your rating before you move on to the rest of your day. This step is easy to defer and easy to forget, and it is worth resisting both impulses.

A rating submitted within an hour of job completion reflects a specific, fresh experience. You remember how the professional communicated during the job, whether they cleaned up after themselves, whether the work matched what was agreed, and how they handled anything unexpected that came up. A rating submitted three days later reflects a general impression, which is useful but less precise and less reliable as feedback.

Your rating also feeds directly into the system that determines which professionals continue to work on the platform and how visible they are to other homeowners. A professional who did the job well deserves a rating that reflects that accurately, because that rating is how they compete for future work. A professional who fell short deserves a rating that reflects that too, because the platform's ability to maintain consistent quality depends on honest feedback from homeowners who were actually there.

This is the step where the circle closes. You hired someone based partly on the ratings left by homeowners before you. The homeowner after you will make the same decision based partly on yours. The rating you leave is not just feedback to the platform. It is the contribution you make to the information quality that makes the whole system trustworthy.

## When Something Feels Off

The steps in this guide are designed for the straightforward situation, which is the vast majority of jobs. But it is worth knowing what to do when something does not feel right, either before or during the job.

If the person at the door does not match the profile, do not let them in. If the professional cannot produce the booking reference or becomes evasive when asked for it, do not let them in. If someone arrives and immediately begins pushing toward starting work before the scope has been agreed, slow the conversation down and return to the walkthrough.

If something feels wrong after work has already started, you have the right to pause it. You do not need a specific articulable reason. If a situation develops that makes you uncomfortable, you can ask the professional to stop, step outside while you contact support through the app, and wait for guidance before proceeding. A professional who responds to this appropriately is operating within the standards the platform holds them to. One who does not is demonstrating exactly why those standards exist.

## The Bottom Line

The ten minutes between opening your door and the moment work begins are the most consequential minutes of any job. The photo check, the booking reference, the scope walkthrough, the pre-work photographs, the access boundaries, the post-job rating. Each one takes less time than it sounds. Together, they create a documented, verified picture of the job from start to finish that protects you clearly and completely regardless of how the work goes.

Most jobs go fine. The professional arrives, does what was agreed, and leaves a space that is better than they found it. The steps in this guide take about ten minutes on a straightforward job and leave no meaningful trace on a good experience. What they do is make the rare difficult situation far easier to resolve, because the documentation exists, the scope was agreed in writing, and the record in the platform is complete.

That is what careful homeownership looks like in practice. Not suspicion. Not friction. Just a clear, repeatable process that works the same way every time and gives you solid ground to stand on whenever you need it.
